# Section Patterns

This file summarizes section-level writing patterns for research papers and review papers.
It is based mainly on Wallwork's chapter logic for Abstract, Introduction, Literature Review, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusions, plus the final-check principles.

## How to use this file

Use this file when the user asks to:
- draft a section from notes
- rewrite a section that is structurally weak
- expand or condense a section
- check whether a section is doing the right job

Do not use this file as a rigid formula.
Use it as a decision aid for what each section must accomplish.

## Global principles

- Each section must have a clear job.
- Do not repeat the same information in the Abstract, Introduction, Discussion, and Conclusions without changing function.
- Keep the referee and reader in mind at all times.
- Prefer clarity, structure, and usefulness over inflated style.
- Highlight contribution with evidence, not adjectives.
- Separate your findings from other people's findings.
- Keep claims within evidential limits.

## Title

### Job

Tell the reader what the paper is about in a specific, readable, searchable way.

### Preferred pattern

- key topic
- specific angle, variable, method, population, or comparison
- if useful, scope or contribution

### Good practices

- keep only information-bearing words
- prefer explicitness over vague praise words
- check journal style for capitalization, punctuation, and length
- make sure title reflects the paper truthfully

### Avoid

- empty boosters such as `novel`, `innovative`, `remarkable`
- strings of nouns that are hard to parse
- overly declarative claims that the paper cannot fully support

## Abstract

### Job

Summarize the whole paper, not just introduce the topic.

### Core pattern

- why the study/review was done
- what was done
- what was found
- what is new or important
- what the implications are

### For research papers

Use a compact sequence:
1. topic/problem
2. aim
3. method/design
4. main results
5. conclusion/implication

### For review papers

Use a compact sequence:
1. review aim
2. selection logic or scope
3. major synthesis findings
4. conclusion
5. implication/future direction

### Abstract checks

- can it stand alone?
- does it summarize all major parts of the paper?
- is background limited to what is necessary?
- are the findings and contribution visible?
- are implications present but not exaggerated?

## Introduction

### Job

Create the logic that makes the paper necessary.

### Core pattern

A typical introduction moves through:
1. topic definition or background
2. accepted state of the art
3. unresolved problem or gap
4. focused literature needed for this paper
5. author's contribution
6. aim of the present work
7. optional main result preview
8. optional implication preview
9. optional paper roadmap

### What the reader should understand by the end

- what problem is being addressed
- why it matters
- what is missing in the current literature
- what this paper intends to do
- what kind of answer the paper will provide

### Paragraph breaks often occur when

- the topic shifts
- the literature focus shifts
- the paper moves from background to gap
- the author states the aim
- the author outlines the paper

### Avoid

- long generic background not tied to the objective
- repeating the abstract
- literature dumping with no link to the paper's goal
- hiding the contribution too late

## Literature Review inside a research paper

### Job

Justify the paper by showing what is known, what is missing, and where the present study fits.

### Core questions

- what are the seminal works?
- what progress has been made?
- what recent works matter most here?
- what are their achievements and limitations?
- what gap remains?
- how does the current paper address that gap?

### Good ordering options

- chronological development
- theme or subtopic
- method or approach
- debate or contradiction
- movement from broad field to precise niche

### Recommended local pattern

1. introduce a subtopic
2. summarize representative studies
3. give a mini-summary of what that subtopic shows
4. move to the next subtopic
5. end by exposing the gap your paper addresses

### Avoid

- citing everything
- citing only literature that supports your line
- paper-by-paper listing with no synthesis
- vague criticism of previous work

## Review Paper Body

### Job

Synthesize the literature into an argument, map, framework, or field-level understanding.

### Core pattern

1. define review focus and scope
2. explain how literature was selected or bounded
3. group studies by logic, not by retrieval order
4. compare findings, methods, assumptions, or periods
5. identify agreement, disagreement, and blind spots
6. connect groups of studies back to the review aim
7. end sections with synthesis, not catalogue

### Good organizing logics

- chronology
- themes
- methods
- populations or datasets
- theories
- controversies
- application areas

### Review-paper requirement

The reader should always know:
- why these studies are being discussed together
- what pattern emerges
- what remains unresolved
- what practical or conceptual value the synthesis provides

## Methods

### Job

Enable readers to understand, evaluate, and if needed replicate what was done.

### Core questions

- what/who was studied?
- where was the study conducted?
- how was the study designed?
- what variables or materials were used?
- what procedures and equipment were used?
- how were data collected and analyzed?
- what assumptions, precautions, difficulties, or modifications matter?

### Preferred structure

1. optional overview of the methodological approach
2. subsections for major stages
3. for each stage:
- preview
- what was done
- why it was done that way
- relevant precautions or modifications
- limitations or practical constraints where needed

### Style guidance

- usually past simple for what was done
- present simple for established methods, standard procedures, software/device descriptions, or formulas where appropriate
- passive is acceptable when focus should stay on the procedure

### Avoid

- missing steps
- unnecessary detail on standard procedures without citation
- list-like sentence chains
- ambiguity in who did what or what happened first

## Results

### Job

Report the findings that answer the research question.

### Core questions

- what was found?
- what was not found?
- what unexpected or contradictory finding matters?

### Preferred structure

1. lead with the most relevant findings
2. organize results in a reader-friendly sequence
3. use figures/tables to support the sequence
4. comment on what the figure means, not just what it contains
5. include negative or contradictory findings if important

### Recommended pattern

- key result
- secondary result
- supporting detail
- contradictory or anomalous result, if relevant

### Style guidance

- usually past simple
- active or passive both possible
- if using present simple to walk the reader through a figure, make it unmistakable that these are your results

### Avoid

- repeating the entire table in prose
- including every result with equal weight
- interpreting too much if Results and Discussion are separate
- hiding findings that do not support the hypothesis

## Discussion

### Job

Interpret the results, relate them to the literature, define their significance, and delimit their meaning.

### Core moves

1. restate or foreground the key finding
2. compare with previous studies
3. explain agreements, contradictions, or mechanisms
4. consider alternative interpretations
5. distinguish supported interpretation from speculation
6. discuss limitations
7. state implications and possible future directions
8. end with the point readers should remember

### Important distinction

Keep clear separation between:
- what the data show
- what you infer from the data
- what remains uncertain

### Strong discussion habits

- make contrasts explicit
- use short, high-signal sentences for important findings
- explain why a result matters rather than calling it important
- mention literature that does not support your position when relevant

### Avoid

- repeating the Results section
- introducing brand-new findings
- over-interpreting the data
- pretending alternative explanations do not exist

## Conclusions

### Job

Leave the reader with the final take-home message and perspective.

### Core pattern

1. restate the main contribution
2. synthesize what is significant
3. explain implication, application, or perspective
4. note future direction if useful
5. keep it concise and distinct from the Abstract and Discussion ending

### Good conclusions do

- say something that becomes meaningful after reading the paper
- add perspective, not mere repetition
- remain specific
- stay moderate in tone

### Avoid

- cut-and-paste from earlier sections
- long methodology recap
- vague generic endings
- unsupported grand implications

## Section-level self-check questions

Before finalizing a section, ask:
- Does this section perform its own function clearly?
- Is it too long for the amount of information it carries?
- Is the contribution visible where it should be?
- Are tense and voice appropriate for the section?
- Is the distinction between my work and prior work always clear?
- Have I cut statements that add no value?
