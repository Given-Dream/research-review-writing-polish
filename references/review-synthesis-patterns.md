# Review Synthesis Patterns

This file supports review-paper writing and literature-review writing.
It is based on Wallwork's treatment of literature reviews, review-paper abstracts and conclusions, and his general principles on clarity, contribution, and reader guidance.

## Core principle

A review is not a pile of citations.
A review must help readers understand:
- what literature matters
- how it has been organized
- what pattern emerges
- what remains unresolved
- why the synthesis is useful

## Use when

Use this file when the user asks to:
- write a review-paper section
- turn literature notes into synthesis prose
- improve a literature review in a research paper
- reduce citation stacking
- make the review more comparative, analytical, or field-shaping

## The minimum architecture of a review

A strong review usually makes the following visible:

1. Aim
- Why is this review being written?
- What question, problem, or field confusion motivates it?

2. Scope or selection logic
- What literature is included and why?
- What is excluded or only briefly covered?

3. Organizing principle
- By chronology
- By theme
- By method
- By debate
- By theory
- By application area
- By population or context

4. Synthesis
- What do these papers collectively show?
- Where do they agree?
- Where do they conflict?
- What limitations recur?

5. Conclusion and implications
- What should the field take away?
- What future work becomes necessary?

## Review abstract pattern

For review papers, the abstract should usually make visible:
- review aim
- selection logic or scope
- synthesis findings
- conclusion
- implications for the field

Do not write a review abstract as if it were only a topic introduction.

## How to structure the review body

## Option A: Chronological story

Use when the field has evolved through clear phases.

Pattern:
1. introduce the subfield or question
2. summarize early foundational work
3. show what changed in later studies
4. identify where progress stalled or fragmented
5. connect to the current state of the field

Use mini-summaries to move from one period to the next.

## Option B: Thematic synthesis

Use when the literature clusters around recurring themes.

Pattern:
1. define the theme
2. present representative studies
3. compare their findings or assumptions
4. identify a consistent pattern or contradiction
5. state what this theme contributes to the overall review argument

## Option C: Method-centered synthesis

Use when methods shape results strongly.

Pattern:
1. introduce the methodological family
2. explain what kinds of questions it can answer
3. compare strengths and weaknesses across studies
4. show what kinds of findings this method tends to produce
5. connect method choice to interpretation limits

## Option D: Debate-centered synthesis

Use when the field contains active disagreement.

Pattern:
1. state the debate clearly
2. summarize the main position
3. summarize the competing position
4. compare evidence and assumptions
5. explain what remains unresolved
6. state your synthesis cautiously

## Local paragraph pattern for review prose

A useful default unit is:

1. topic sentence
- define the paragraph's comparison point or claim

2. grouped evidence
- present multiple studies as a cluster, not as isolated items

3. synthesis sentence
- state what the cluster collectively suggests

4. boundary sentence
- explain limitation, exception, contradiction, or transition

Example logic:
- Studies in this line generally report X.
- However, they differ in method, context, or interpretation.
- Taken together, they suggest Y, but leave Z unresolved.

## Author-focused vs idea-focused citation

Wallwork highlights that citation style changes emphasis.

Use author-focused citation when:
- comparing specific scholars
- showing development from one study to another
- building a chronological narrative

Use idea-focused citation when:
- the finding matters more than the name
- the paragraph should foreground concepts rather than authors
- too many author names would make the prose heavy

Vary citation style to avoid monotony and to control emphasis.

## How to talk about limitations of previous work

Be constructive and precise.

Prefer:
- identifying what has not yet been addressed
- specifying context, method, sample, or variable limitations
- showing conflicting findings
- stating that attention has not yet been paid to a relevant aspect

Avoid:
- dismissive claims
- vague statements like `previous studies are insufficient`
- overstating your novelty without support

Good limitation moves:
- previous studies focus on X, whereas Y remains underexplored
- results across studies are not fully consistent
- most studies examine one context, leaving another untested
- one relevant dimension has received little attention so far

## How to connect the literature to your own review argument

At regular intervals, make explicit:
- why these studies were grouped
- what they collectively imply
- how the current section advances the review aim

Do not assume the reader will infer the connection.

## How to conclude a review paper

A review conclusion should:
- restate why the review was needed
- summarize the main synthesis findings
- connect those findings back to the review aim
- explain implications for the field
- propose realistic future directions
- end with a final perspective, not with a loose citation list

## Anti-patterns

Avoid:
- one-study-per-sentence catalogues with no synthesis
- broad claims that the selected literature cannot support
- citing only papers that confirm one preferred view
- discussing papers in an arbitrary order
- repeating the same claim in slightly different words
- turning paraphrase into plagiarism
- using AI paraphrase tools to disguise source wording without attribution

## Review self-check

Ask:
- Have I made the organizing logic explicit?
- Is it clear why these studies are discussed together?
- Do I synthesize rather than list?
- Have I mentioned both supporting and non-supporting literature where needed?
- Is the order of papers logical?
- Have I shown what gap, controversy, or trend emerges?
- Are my conclusions more than a restatement of section headings?
- Have I acknowledged sources whenever ideas are not originally mine?

## Useful sentence functions

Use these functions, not as fixed phrases, but as rhetorical moves:
- introduce a line of work
- summarize a representative cluster
- mark a shift in period, theme, or method
- state convergence
- state divergence
- expose a limitation
- identify a gap
- connect the gap to the review aim
- close a section with synthesis
